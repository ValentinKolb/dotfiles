# Academic prose review

Read only the section matching the document language, plus the shared review
sequence. These are judgment prompts, not mechanical rules.

## Shared review sequence

Review prose after the argument and structure are sound:

1. Give each paragraph one discernible function in the argument.
2. Put the topic or logical connection early enough to orient the reader.
3. Keep the subject, main verb, and essential claim easy to find.
4. Prefer verbs that name the action over abstract noun constructions when the
   rewrite remains idiomatic in the field.
5. Remove filler, duplicated qualification, and meta-commentary that adds no
   claim or transition.
6. Use one stable term for one concept. Do not vary technical vocabulary merely
   to avoid repetition.
7. Check pronouns, demonstratives, and transitions for an unambiguous referent
   and real logical relationship.
8. Vary sentence shape when it improves emphasis and readability; do not chase
   a universal sentence-length threshold.
9. Preserve necessary qualification, uncertainty, and disciplinary
   terminology.
10. Read the revised paragraph as an argument, not just as isolated correct
    sentences.

For each material issue, show the original, a concrete revision, and why it is
better. Group recurring minor issues rather than listing every occurrence.

## Signal and emphasis

Make the main point easy to find. Academic rigor does not require the author to
circle a topic before stating what matters. Move the claim or paragraph purpose
forward, then retain only the context the reader needs to understand or assess
it. An introduction may build toward a research question, but every sentence in
that path still needs a clear job.

Weight the text according to the argument:

- give the research question, decisive reasoning, strongest evidence,
  uncertainty, and limitations the space they need;
- shorten familiar background, procedural narration, and examples that no
  longer change the conclusion;
- do not confuse length, repetition, or emphatic wording with importance;
- do not give minor qualifications the same prominence as a central defect;
- make the heading, opening, and conclusion agree about what the section is
  actually about.

For each sentence, ask what it contributes: a claim, evidence, reasoning,
qualification, definition, or necessary transition. Remove it when it performs
none of these functions. Merge sentences that repeat the same contribution in
different words. Preserve repetition only when it deliberately restores
context, contrasts positions, or emphasizes a result that warrants it.

## Lists and parallel points

Use a list only when the items are easier to compare or follow separately than
in prose. Before writing one, identify the single question or category that all
items answer.

A useful list has:

- distinct items rather than several phrasings of the same claim;
- one conceptual level, without mixing a broad category with its examples;
- parallel grammar when that makes the relationship clearer;
- an order based on importance, logic, chronology, or another stated criterion;
- enough explanation to understand each item, but no repeated preamble.

Merge overlaps before adding detail. If ten proposed bullets contain four real
claims, write four bullets. Do not split one argument across several items just
to make the response look comprehensive. Conversely, do not compress genuinely
different claims into a vague umbrella term.

Apply the same test to enumerations in continuous prose. A sentence containing
several nouns or clauses can hide the same overlap, mixed levels, or missing
ordering as a bad bullet list.

## Generic and formulaic prose

Run this audit after the substantive edit. The goal is specific, appropriate
academic prose, not avoiding an AI detector.

Check for:

- significance signals without support, such as “groundbreaking”, “pivotal”,
  “of central importance”, or “von besonderer Relevanz”;
- vague attribution, such as “research shows”, “experts argue”, or “es wird
  allgemein angenommen”, when a concrete source or narrower statement is
  required;
- ornamental transitions or participial endings that imply a logical relation
  without establishing one;
- formulaic contrasts such as “not only X but also Y” when the direct claim is
  clearer;
- forced groups of three, false ranges, and symmetric pros-and-cons structures
  that do not reflect the evidence;
- promotional, chatbot-like, congratulatory, or sycophantic language;
- stock introductions and conclusions that could be moved unchanged into an
  unrelated academic text;
- fashionable or elaborate vocabulary used where a plain, precise term would
  say more;
- a polished but anonymous voice that erases the author's real position,
  disciplinary conventions, or degree of certainty.

Use the swap test: if a sentence could appear unchanged in almost any seminar
paper, ask what concrete claim, relation, or scope it contributes. Make it
specific or remove it.

Do not extend the explicit `U+2014 EM DASH` prohibition into broad punctuation
bans. A term, colon, parenthesis, en dash, passive construction, or long
sentence can be exactly right in context. Correct the failed function, not the
superficial marker.

Before delivering revised prose, check the full output for `U+2014 EM DASH` and
the invisible characters `U+200B`, `U+200C`, `U+200D`, `U+2060`, and `U+FEFF`.
Rewrite or remove them unless they occur inside an exact quotation, code
literal, identifier, or source excerpt that must remain unchanged. In that
case, preserve and flag the source character.

## Tone and authorial voice

Match the tone to the assignment, discipline, audience, and author's existing
voice. Academic tone is precise and proportionate; it is not automatically
impersonal, inflated, emotionless, or uniform.

Avoid manufacturing personality through casual asides, opinions unsupported by
the argument, deliberate messiness, or first person where the convention does
not permit it. Keep first person when it accurately clarifies the author's
action or position and fits the context. Preserve productive uncertainty and
disagreement without turning every issue into artificial balance.

## German prose

Clear German academic writing need not sound bureaucratic. Prefer precise,
idiomatic statements over signals of importance such as “Es ist festzuhalten,
dass” or “An dieser Stelle soll darauf hingewiesen werden, dass”. Delete the
frame when the proposition can stand on its own.

Check especially:

- long sentence openings that delay the main proposition;
- noun-heavy constructions where a verb makes the actor and action clearer;
- passive or impersonal constructions that conceal responsibility;
- chains of genitives and nested attributes that overload the noun phrase;
- vague references such as “dies”, “dabei”, “hierdurch”, or “in diesem
  Zusammenhang” without a clear antecedent;
- transition words whose claimed relationship does not hold;
- unnecessary Anglicisms or translated English syntax;
- inconsistent compounds, capitalization, abbreviations, and gender-inclusive
  forms within the chosen house style.

Do not reject passive voice or nominal style categorically. Both can be useful
when the process, result, or concept matters more than the actor. Avoid replacing
established technical nouns with awkward paraphrases.

Do not make the prose colloquial in the name of simplicity. Preserve the
distinction between accessible language and casual language.

## English prose

Prefer direct subjects and specific verbs. Consider active voice when it makes
the actor or responsibility clearer, but retain passive voice when the actor is
unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately backgrounded.

Check especially:

- dead-weight frames such as “it is important to note that”;
- nominalizations that hide a useful action, such as “perform an analysis of”
  where “analyze” is clearer;
- subjects separated from their main verbs by long interruptions;
- stacked nouns, misplaced modifiers, and unclear pronoun references;
- unnecessary synonym variation for defined concepts;
- paragraphs whose sentences are grammatically correct but do not progress
  logically;
- inconsistent dialect, spelling, capitalization, abbreviations, and
  hyphenation.

Do not convert every passive sentence, eliminate every nominalization, or force
every sentence into the same rhythm. Academic prose needs both precision and
variation. Preserve quotations exactly unless the user is authorized to edit
them.

When a substantial English draft exists as a local file, finish with the
optional Harper pass described in `SKILL.md`. Harper supplements this review;
it does not decide whether an academic sentence is correct.

## Learning-oriented feedback

When the author wants to improve their writing rather than only repair the
current text:

- identify at most three recurring habits at a time;
- explain the effect on the reader;
- contrast a representative before/after example;
- give the author a reusable self-check;
- preserve successful passages and say why they work.

Avoid burying the author under a complete catalog of stylistic preferences.
